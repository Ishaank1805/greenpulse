## Role 1 (sampling)

Sources: `data/results/b2_checks.csv`, `data/results/run_log.csv`, `data/samples/*.csv`.

- **All 303 cities were sampled in 3 seasons with no failures:** 53,614 squares of 1 km, of which 26,341 are urban (built ≥ 10%) with a daytime temperature. 202 cities have at least 30 such squares.
- **Sampling matches the grid:** the median city has exactly the number of squares its area predicts (ratio 1.00), and the pre-monsoon values reproduce the Jodhpur/Kochi pilot exactly.
- **Cloud thins the data but rarely empties a square.** The median city has a clear daytime image 98% of the time before the monsoon, 95% after it and 94% in winter. Each season has its own cloudy region: Kerala before the monsoon (Kottayam 46%), Tamil Nadu after it (Viluppuram 37%), and the Gangetic plain in winter (Jaunpur 60%, likely fog).
- **Squares with no temperature are water, not cloud:** the missing share is identical in all three seasons in every city (worst Vadakara 19%, Thalassery 18%, Mangaluru 17%, all coastal).
- **Missing squares are the same squares in every season** in all 303 cities, which confirms they are water (MODIS does not report land temperature over water), not cloud.
- **Seasons differ strongly overall but not everywhere:** the median square is 39.7 °C by day before the monsoon, 31.2 °C after it and 27.9 °C in winter. In Kochi, winter (34.1 °C) is slightly warmer than pre-monsoon (33.7 °C), because Kerala's April–May is already wet: Kochi gets 622 mm of rain in April–May against 126 mm in winter, while the median city gets 67 mm in April–May (`city_climate.csv`).
- **Hill cities reach sub-zero surfaces:** winter night surface temperature falls to −12.8 °C (Khansahib), −7.9 °C (Kralpora) and −6.5 °C (Srinagar) in the Kashmir valley, at 1,600–2,000 m. These were flagged by the range checks and confirmed as real.

## Role 2 (climate)

Sources: `data/results/city_climate.csv`, `main_result_regressions.csv`, `main_result_bins.csv`, `robustness.csv` (night_warming rows), and the printed checks of `07_city_climate.py` and `08_main_result.py`. Final run (G4): main spec with built-up, elevation and water controls. Slopes are °C per 0.1 NDVI; negative = greener squares are cooler. Rainfall effects are given per 10× rainfall (log10 scale). The cities with estimates span 262 mm (Bikaner) to 3,635 mm (Shillong), a little more than one tenfold step, so a slope per 10× rain is roughly the difference between the driest and wettest cities. Associations, not causes.

- **Climate data:** ERA5-Land 2019–2024 for all 303 cities (909 city × season rows, none missing). Annual rainfall runs from 262 mm (Bikaner) to 3,792 mm (Darjeeling); the pilot cities are Jodhpur 411 mm and Kochi 3,131 mm. ERA5 reads Kochi slightly cool (27.9 °C pre-monsoon), as expected of a 9 km value on the coast. Puri's thin coastal outline misses every ERA5 land cell, so its climate comes from a 10 km buffer around its centre.
- **Humidity changes felt heat (sets up H6):** before the monsoon the Heat Index is 6–7.5 °C above the daily maximum temperature in humid coastal cities (Chennai +7.5, Kolkata +7.2, Kochi +6.3, Mumbai +5.8). In dry Jodhpur it is 1.4 °C *below* it.
- **H2, by day: no clear link with rainfall.** Across 202 cities the daytime cooling slope shows no clear trend with annual rainfall in any season: pre-monsoon +0.17 (95% CI −0.73 to +1.07), post-monsoon −0.02 (−1.51 to +1.46), winter −0.09 (−0.94 to +0.75). Humidity, soil moisture, air temperature and the Heat Index show no clear link either (pre-monsoon humidity: +0.001 per 1% RH, 95% CI −0.008 to +0.010). The pre-monsoon result holds unweighted, with capped weights, without the few-block cities, and with city size, air temperature and density in the model. So by day in Indian cities we cannot support either side of the debate: neither Guo 2026 / Schwaab 2021 (weaker cooling in dry climates) nor Manoli 2019 / Yang 2022 (stronger).
- **One caveat on the daytime result:** without the cities that rest on 5 or fewer blocks, wetter cities cool slightly more after the monsoon (−0.60, 95% CI −1.05 to −0.15) and in winter (−0.44, −0.80 to −0.08). Leaving out Kannur or Alappuzha alone flips the sign of the pre-monsoon slope. Any daytime rainfall effect is small and fragile.
- **Threshold, dry cities still cool by day:** of 56 cities under 1,000 mm, 0% show significant greener-is-warmer days before the monsoon and 77% significantly cool (median −1.06). For the 146 wetter cities the figures are 3% and 74% (median −1.18). Guo et al. 2026 found 22% net warming under 1,000 mm. One possible reason, from Manoli 2019, is that urban greenery in dry Indian cities is irrigated, which rainfall cannot see.
- **H3, at night: the slope reverses in dry cities.** At night the cooling slope is clearly more negative in wetter cities before the monsoon (−1.16 per 10× rain, 95% CI −1.75 to −0.57, 135 cities) and after it (−0.98, −1.57 to −0.40, 141 cities). The result holds unweighted, with capped weights, without the few-block cities, and with city size added. Before the monsoon, 38% of 39 dry cities have significantly greener-is-warmer nights against 18% of 96 wet cities. The share falls steadily with rainfall: 50% under 750 mm (10 cities), 28% at 750–1,500 mm, 3% over 1,500 mm. So Jodhpur's warm green nights belong to a dry-city pattern, which fits Guo's heat-storage idea.
- **Temperature and density do not explain the night pattern:** with air temperature and built-up density added, the rainfall effect remains before the monsoon (−0.92, 95% CI −1.70 to −0.14) and after it (−0.77, −1.37 to −0.16). Before the water control was added, the pre-monsoon effect did not survive this test. City size is never significant in any panel.
- **Season contrast:** by day the rainfall pattern is flat in all three seasons. At night the dry-city warming is strongest before the monsoon, weaker after it (25% of 44 dry vs 10% of 97 wet cities), and gone in winter (11% vs 11%; winter night rainfall slope +0.28, 95% CI −0.14 to +0.70).
- **Extreme cities are mostly small cities:** the four most negative pre-monsoon day slopes come from cities with 32–46 squares (Sagar, Davanagere, Dharwad, Dhule; the fifth is Udaipur, 104). The most positive come from cities with 30–41 squares (Bharatpur, Tezpur, Thoothukudi, Mirzapur; the fifth is Patna, 177).
- **Limits:** ERA5 is ~9 km, so it gives each city's background climate, not differences inside the city. The Heat Index uses each day's maximum temperature with its mean dewpoint, which approximates the hottest hour. Rainfall cannot see irrigation (Manoli 2019).

## Role 3 (pilot, seasons, robustness)

Sources: `data/results/cooling_efficiency.csv` (main spec via `common.main_spec`: NDVI, built ≥ 10%, built + elevation + water; final run G4 with Role 5's water files), `dropped_cities.csv`, `seasons.csv`, `seasons_summary.csv`, `robustness.csv`, `robustness_ndvi_evi.csv`. Slopes are °C per 0.1 NDVI; negative = greener squares are cooler. Associations, not causes.

- **H1, greener is cooler by day almost everywhere:** before the monsoon, 151 of 202 cities (75%) show significantly cooler greener squares, and 191 (95%) have a negative slope. The median is −1.13 °C per 0.1 NDVI. After the monsoon: 142 of 202 (70%), median −0.67. Winter: 150 of 202 (74%), median −0.81. Significantly greener-is-warmer days are rare (2%).
- **Pilot reproduced exactly:** the 3-season script reproduces every pilot row (ce, se, n, blocks) to 1e-13: Jodhpur −1.32 and Kochi −1.04 at the pilot spec. In the final spec Jodhpur is −1.45 (water changes nothing there) and Kochi −1.90.
- **Why Kochi strengthens:** water squares are cool (−5.7 °C per unit water share) and have low NDVI, so without the water control low-greenery squares looked cooler and the cooling was understated. Kochi's odd elevation effect (+10 °C per 100 m in a 1–38 m city) falls to +7.5 once water is added (elevation and water correlate at −0.43). So elevation partly stood in for distance from the backwaters, but not entirely.
- **The water control changes little overall (G4):** across all cities the ranking with and without water correlates 0.91–0.97, and the median slope moves by at most 0.02. The share of cities with significant daytime cooling rises from 70% to 75% before the monsoon. The biggest single-city changes are in Rishikesh (2.5 by day before the monsoon) and Darbhanga. Dropping the 1.9% of squares that are more than half water changes nothing (ρ = 1.00).
- **H3, nights:** night slopes centre on zero (median +0.08 pre, −0.07 post, −0.19 winter; 135 / 141 / 157 cities), which fits Yang 2022 and Du 2024 (night cooling near zero). Before the monsoon, slightly more cities have significantly greener-is-warmer nights (32, 24%) than greener-is-cooler (25, 19%). Split by rainfall, before the monsoon **38% of 39 dry cities** (< 1,000 mm) have warmer green nights against **18% of 96 wet cities**. After the monsoon it is 25% of 44 vs 10% of 97; in winter, 11% vs 11%.
- **Night data are thinner for humid cities:** wet cities lose more night estimates to cloud (pre / post / winter: 34% / 34% / 25% of wet cities vs 30% / 21% / 16% of dry). With a 6-night cut-off the result holds and gets stronger: before the monsoon 54% of 48 dry cities vs 18% of 119 wet. The ranking barely changes (ρ 0.91–0.95).
- **H3 depends on the built-up control:** without controls, greener squares look significantly cooler at night in 53–75% of cities. With the controls, most of that disappears: greener squares are less built-up, and built-up surfaces hold heat at night. The night rank correlation with the main spec is only 0.25–0.58 without controls.
- **H4, seasons, same city:** by day, cooling is **weaker** after the monsoon in 81% of 202 cities (median change +0.43 °C per 0.1 NDVI; Wilcoxon p ≈ 7e-19). Only 2% are significantly stronger, against 30% significantly weaker. Winter is also weaker than pre-monsoon (median +0.32). At night the reverse: cooling is stronger after the monsoon in 69% of 127 cities (median −0.16, p ≈ 3e-5), and in winter in 66% of 126 (median −0.18). One possible reading: pre-monsoon days have the most sun and the hottest surfaces (median square 39.7 °C vs 31.2 °C post-monsoon, Role 1), so there is more heat for vegetation to offset. This is a hypothesis, not tested yet.
- **Soil wetting does not clearly explain the change:** the post − pre change has no clear relation to how much soils wetted (−1.71 per m³/m³, 95% CI −3.65 to +0.24, p = 0.09, 202 cities). Dry and wet cities weaken alike (median +0.49 under 1,000 mm, +0.42 above). So Guo's idea that dry cities gain most when soils are wet is not supported here. The standard error of the change treats the seasons as independent, which is conservative.
- **Robustness (pre-monsoon, day):** the daytime result holds under every variant (70–77% of cities significantly cooling). The variant that matters most is **dropping the controls**: the city ranking correlates only 0.56 with the main spec. EVI gives a similar ranking (ρ 0.83). A 20% built-up cut-off keeps the pattern but loses 82 cities. **The rainfall slope has no stable sign:** it ranges from −0.71 to +3.61 across variants and none is significant at the 5% level, which supports reporting "no clear daytime pattern".

- **NDVI vs EVI:** the two disagree on significance in 14% of 202 cities (pre/day). The pilot's explanation (NDVI tightly tied to built-up share, as in Kochi) does not generalise: disagreement is 16% where r(NDVI, built) < −0.7 and 13% elsewhere (Fisher p = 0.54). By rainfall: 16% of 56 dry cities, 14% of 146 wet ones.
- **Data limits:** 101 of 303 cities have fewer than 30 urban squares and get no estimate in any season. At night a further 67 / 61 / 45 cities (pre / post / winter) lose their estimate to cloud. No post-monsoon day estimates are lost, so the season comparison keeps all 202 cities by day.
- **Uncertainty in small cities (for the team):** 21% of day estimates and 26% of night estimates rest on 5 or fewer 5 km blocks. Clustered standard errors from so few blocks are unreliable and often too small, so these cities get extra weight in the 1/SE² regressions: 22% of the pre/day weight. No single city dominates (the largest is Darbhanga, 4.4%). Script 08 also reports the rainfall slopes with capped weights and without these cities; the night results hold both ways.

## Role 4 (exposure)

Source: `data/results/exposure.csv`. 202 cities with ≥ 30 urban squares (built ≥ 10%).

- **Crowded squares are less green almost everywhere.** People-weighted NDVI is below the area average in 90% (pre), 95% (post) and 96% (winter) of 202 cities.
- **At night, people live in hotter squares in almost every city:** 96% (pre, n = 135), 96% (post, n = 141) and 97% (winter, n = 157). The median gap is +0.18 to +0.22 °C. Humid cities are under-represented at night because of cloud.
- **By day, the pattern is weaker and depends on season:** people live in hotter squares in 63% (pre), 87% (post) and 73% (winter) of cities. The median gap is +0.09, +0.18 and +0.12 °C.
- **Where crowded squares are less green, they are hotter.** Across cities, the greenness gap and the daytime heat gap are strongly linked: Spearman ρ = −0.75 (pre), −0.72 (post), −0.74 (winter), all p < 1e-30. This ties the exposure result to vegetation: the cities that put the least greenery where people live are the ones where people get the most extra heat.
- **Rainfall matters only a little, and only before the monsoon.** Before the monsoon, people live in hotter-than-average squares in 55% of 56 dry cities (< 1,000 mm, median gap +0.03 °C) against 66% of 146 wet ones (+0.11 °C). Spearman ρ between gap and rainfall is +0.19 (p = 0.006). After the monsoon (88% vs 86%) and in winter (73% vs 73%) there is no difference.
- **The pre-monsoon exceptions are not mainly dry cities.** Among the 20 largest, people live in *cooler*-than-average squares by day in Surat (−0.72 °C), Pune (−0.66), Mumbai (−0.47), Kozhikode (−0.39), Indore (−0.34), Nagpur (−0.30) and Jaipur (−0.25). Only Jaipur is under 1,000 mm. So the pilot's explanation for Jodhpur (bare outskirts hotter than the city) does not obviously carry over, and the reason is not established.
- **Gaps are small on average** (tenths of a degree) **but reach about 1 °C** in some cities (Kolkata +1.08 °C pre-monsoon day). Kolkata is also the city where crowded squares are least green (−0.05 NDVI).
- **Comparison with the literature:** Chakraborty et al. 2019 found poorer neighbourhoods hotter in 72% of 25 cities, using surface temperature. We use population density, not income, so we can say "more crowded squares", not "poorer". Our 63–87% by day is in the same range.

## Role 5 (water and data quality)

- **Monsoon availability:** In Mumbai, usable daytime LST composites fall from **85.0% in April–May to 25.2% in June–September**, supporting exclusion of the monsoon from the main seasonal analysis (`data/results/lst_availability_by_month.csv`).
- **Water control:** Across **202 cities**, the median pre-monsoon daytime NDVI slope changes only from **−1.052 to −1.129 °C per 0.1 NDVI** after controlling for mapped water share, so water does not explain away the overall cooling association (`data/results/cooling_efficiency.csv`).
- **Jodhpur night:** Jodhpur's pre-monsoon nighttime slope remains **+0.944 → +0.944 °C per 0.1 NDVI** after adding the water control, so its greener-is-warmer night association is not explained by mapped water (`data/results/cooling_efficiency.csv`).
