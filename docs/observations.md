## Role 1 (sampling)

Sources: `data/results/b2_checks.csv`, `data/results/run_log.csv`, `data/samples/*.csv`.

- **All 303 cities were sampled in 3 seasons with no failures:** 53,614 squares of 1 km, of which 26,341 are urban (built ≥ 10%) with a daytime temperature. 202 cities have at least 30 such squares.
- **Sampling matches the grid:** the median city has exactly the number of squares its area predicts (ratio 1.00), and the pre-monsoon values reproduce the Jodhpur/Kochi pilot exactly.
- **Cloud thins the data but rarely empties a square.** The median city has a clear daytime image 98% of the time before the monsoon, 95% after it and 94% in winter. Each season has its own cloudy region: Kerala before the monsoon (Kottayam 46%), Tamil Nadu after it (Viluppuram 37%), and the Gangetic plain in winter (Jaunpur 60%, likely fog).
- **Squares with no temperature are water, not cloud:** the missing share is identical in all three seasons in every city (worst Vadakara 19%, Thalassery 18%, Mangaluru 17%, all coastal).
- **Seasons differ strongly overall but not everywhere:** the median square is 39.7 °C by day before the monsoon, 31.2 °C after it and 27.9 °C in winter. In Kochi, winter (34.1 °C) is slightly warmer than pre-monsoon (33.7 °C), because Kerala's April–May is already wet. Role 2's `city_climate.csv` can confirm the rainfall.
- **Hill cities reach sub-zero surfaces:** winter night surface temperature falls to −12.8 °C (Khansahib), −7.9 °C (Kralpora) and −6.5 °C (Srinagar) in the Kashmir valley, at 1,600–2,000 m. These were flagged by the range checks and confirmed as real.

## Role 2 (climate)

Sources: `data/results/city_climate.csv`, `main_result_regressions.csv`, `main_result_bins.csv`, `robustness.csv` (night_warming rows), and the printed checks of `07_city_climate.py` and `08_main_result.py`. Slopes are °C per 0.1 NDVI; negative = greener squares are cooler. Rainfall effects are given per 10× rainfall (log10 scale). Our cities span about 400 to 3,100 mm, which is just under one tenfold step. Associations, not causes.

- **Climate data:** ERA5-Land 2019–2024 for all 303 cities (909 city × season rows, none missing). Annual rainfall runs from 411 mm (Jodhpur) to 3,131 mm (Kochi). ERA5 reads Kochi slightly cool (27.9 °C pre-monsoon), as expected of a 9 km value on the coast. Puri's thin coastal outline misses every ERA5 land cell, so its climate comes from a 10 km buffer around its centre.
- **Humidity changes felt heat (sets up H6):** before the monsoon the Heat Index is 6–7.5 °C above the daily maximum temperature in humid coastal cities (Chennai +7.5, Kolkata +7.2, Kochi +6.3, Mumbai +5.8). In dry Jodhpur it is 1.4 °C *below* it.
- **H2, by day: no clear link with rainfall.** Across 202 cities the daytime cooling slope shows no clear trend with annual rainfall in any season: pre-monsoon +0.41 (95% CI −0.56 to +1.37), post-monsoon −0.39 (−0.80 to +0.03), winter +0.36 (−1.00 to +1.72). The same holds unweighted, with capped weights, without the cities that rest on few blocks, and with air temperature and density in the model. So by day in Indian cities we cannot support either side of the debate: neither Guo 2026 / Schwaab 2021 (weaker cooling in dry climates) nor Manoli 2019 / Yang 2022 (stronger).
- **Threshold, dry cities still cool by day:** of 56 cities under 1,000 mm, 0% show significant greener-is-warmer days before the monsoon and 73% significantly cool (median −1.02). For the 146 wetter cities the figures are 3% and 68% (median −1.10). Guo et al. 2026 found 22% net warming under 1,000 mm. One possible reason, from Manoli 2019, is that urban greenery in dry Indian cities is irrigated, which rainfall cannot see.
- **H3, at night: the slope reverses in dry cities.** At night the cooling slope is clearly more negative in wetter cities before the monsoon (−1.11 per 10× rain, 95% CI −1.78 to −0.45, 135 cities) and after it (−1.02, −1.69 to −0.36, 141 cities). The result holds unweighted, with capped weights, and without the few-block cities. Before the monsoon, 49% of 39 dry cities have significantly greener-is-warmer nights against 16% of 96 wet cities. The share falls steadily with rainfall: 50% under 750 mm (10 cities), 30% at 750–1,500 mm, 3% over 1,500 mm. So Jodhpur's warm green nights belong to a dry-city pattern, which fits Guo's heat-storage idea.
- **City size does not explain it:** adding city size (log of the number of urban squares) leaves every rainfall result unchanged. By day, still no clear trend (pre +0.40, 95% CI −0.70 to +1.50); at night the effect stays clear before the monsoon (−1.10, −1.90 to −0.30) and after it (−1.10, −1.83 to −0.37). Size itself is never significant in any of the six panels.
- **What else could explain the night pattern:** with air temperature and built-up density added, the post-monsoon night effect remains (−0.89, 95% CI −1.70 to −0.07). The pre-monsoon effect shrinks to −0.51 and is no longer clear (−1.53 to +0.50). Before the monsoon, the rainfall link at night overlaps with heat and density, and we cannot separate them.
- **Season contrast:** by day the rainfall pattern is flat in all three seasons. At night the dry-city warming is strongest before the monsoon, weaker after it (25% of 44 dry vs 7% of 97 wet cities), and nearly gone in winter (15% of 47 vs 9% of 110; winter night rainfall slope +0.25, 95% CI −0.24 to +0.75).
- **Soil moisture:** no reliable signal. In winter by day the weighted and unweighted fits even disagree in sign (+0.44 vs −1.62).
- **Extreme cities are small cities:** the five most negative and five most positive pre-monsoon day slopes all come from cities with 30–46 squares (e.g. Dhule, Sagar; Rishikesh, Tezpur), except Patna (177 squares). No single city flips the sign of the rainfall result; the most influential is Roorkee, which shifts it by 0.29.
- **Limits:** ERA5 is ~9 km, so it gives each city's background climate, not differences inside the city. The Heat Index uses each day's maximum temperature with its mean dewpoint, which approximates the hottest hour. Rainfall cannot see irrigation (Manoli 2019).

## Role 3 (pilot, seasons, robustness)

Sources: `data/results/cooling_efficiency.csv` (main spec via `common.main_spec`: NDVI, built ≥ 10%, built + elevation; no water files yet), `dropped_cities.csv`, `seasons.csv`, `seasons_summary.csv`, `robustness.csv`, `robustness_ndvi_evi.csv`. Slopes are °C per 0.1 NDVI; negative = greener squares are cooler. Associations, not causes.

- **H1, greener is cooler by day almost everywhere:** before the monsoon, 141 of 202 cities (70%) show significantly cooler greener squares, and 188 (93%) have a negative slope. The median is −1.05 °C per 0.1 NDVI. After the monsoon: 138 of 202 (68%), median −0.63. Winter: 140 of 202 (69%), median −0.77. Significantly greener-is-warmer days are rare (2–3%).
- **Pilot reproduced exactly:** the 3-season script reproduces every pilot row (ce, se, n, blocks) to 1e-13. Jodhpur −1.32 and Kochi −1.04 at the pilot spec. Adding elevation (the main spec) moves both to −1.44. In Kochi (1–38 m) the elevation coefficient is +10 °C per 100 m, far from a lapse-rate effect: there elevation most likely stands in for distance from the backwaters. The water control (Role 5) should test this.
- **H3, nights:** night slopes centre on zero (median +0.09 pre, −0.13 post, −0.25 winter; 135 / 141 / 157 cities), which fits Yang 2022 and Du 2024 (night cooling near zero). Split by rainfall, before the monsoon **49% of 39 dry cities** (< 1,000 mm) have significantly greener-is-warmer nights against **16% of 96 wet cities**. After the monsoon it is 25% of 44 vs 7% of 97, and in winter 15% of 47 vs 9% of 110.
- **Night data are thinner for humid cities:** wet cities lose more night estimates to cloud (pre / post / winter: 34% / 34% / 25% of wet cities vs 30% / 21% / 16% of dry). With a 6-night cut-off the result holds and gets stronger: before the monsoon 60% of 48 dry cities vs 16% of 119 wet.
- **H3 depends on the built-up control:** without controls, greener squares look cooler at night in 53–75% of cities. With the built-up control, most of that disappears: greener squares are less built-up, and built-up surfaces hold heat at night. The night rank correlation with the main spec is only 0.27–0.58 without controls.
- **H4, seasons, same city:** by day, cooling is **weaker** after the monsoon in 79% of 202 cities (median change +0.45 °C per 0.1 NDVI; Wilcoxon p ≈ 2e-17). Only 4% are significantly stronger, against 26% significantly weaker. Winter is also weaker than pre-monsoon (median +0.31). At night the reverse: cooling is stronger after the monsoon in 73% of 127 cities (median −0.18, p ≈ 7e-7). One possible reading: pre-monsoon days have the most sun and the hottest surfaces (median square 39.7 °C vs 31.2 °C post-monsoon, Role 1), so there is more heat for vegetation to offset. This is a hypothesis, not tested yet. **Soil wetting does not explain the change:** the post − pre change is unrelated to how much soils wetted (−0.08 per m³/m³, 95% CI −2.91 to +2.74, 202 cities). Dry and wet cities weaken alike (median +0.49 under 1,000 mm, +0.45 above). So Guo's idea that dry cities gain most when soils are wet is not supported here. Winter nights, like post-monsoon nights, are cooler-when-greener than pre-monsoon nights (median −0.20, 67% of 126 cities). The standard error of the change treats the seasons as independent, which is conservative.
- **Robustness (pre-monsoon, day):** the daytime result holds under every variant (66–77% of cities significantly cooling). The variant that matters most is **dropping the controls**: the city ranking correlates only 0.60 with the main spec. EVI gives the same ranking (ρ 0.85). A 20% built-up cut-off keeps the pattern but loses 82 cities. **The rainfall slope has no stable sign:** it ranges from −0.58 to +3.61 across variants and is significant only with EVI (+1.42, p = 0.04, i.e. stronger EVI cooling in drier cities). One variant out of six is not enough to build on, which supports reporting "no clear daytime pattern".

- **NDVI vs EVI:** the two disagree on significance in 13% of 202 cities (pre/day). The pilot's explanation (NDVI tightly tied to built-up share, as in Kochi) does not generalise: disagreement is 14% where r(NDVI, built) < −0.7 and 12% elsewhere (Fisher p = 0.83). By rainfall: 9% of 56 dry cities, 14% of 146 wet ones.
- **Night data with a 6-night cut-off:** this recovers 32 / 26 / 24 cities (pre / post / winter) and leaves the night picture unchanged (rank correlation 0.93–0.94; pre-monsoon warming share 29% vs 25%).
- **Data limits:** 101 of 303 cities have fewer than 30 urban squares and get no estimate in any season. At night a further 67 / 61 / 45 cities (pre / post / winter) lose their estimate to cloud. No post-monsoon day estimates are lost, so the season comparison keeps all 202 cities by day.
- **Uncertainty in small cities (for the team):** 21% of day estimates and 26% of night estimates rest on 5 or fewer 5 km blocks. Clustered standard errors from so few blocks are unreliable and often too small. So these cities get large weights in the 1/SE² regressions: Roorkee (36 squares, 4 blocks) carries 9% of the pre/day weight, more than New Delhi (128 blocks). Script 08 therefore also reports the rainfall slope with capped weights and without these cities.

## Role 4 (exposure)

Source: `data/results/exposure.csv`. 202 cities with ≥ 30 urban squares (built ≥ 10%).

- **Crowded squares are less green almost everywhere.** People-weighted NDVI is below the area average in 90% (pre), 95% (post) and 96% (winter) of 202 cities.
- **At night, people live in hotter squares in almost every city:** 96% (pre, n = 135), 96% (post, n = 141) and 97% (winter, n = 157). The median gap is +0.18 to +0.22 °C. Humid cities are under-represented at night because of cloud.
- **By day, the pattern is weaker and depends on season:** people live in hotter squares in 63% (pre), 87% (post) and 73% (winter) of cities. The median gap is +0.09, +0.18 and +0.12 °C.
- **The pre-monsoon exceptions include hot, dry-season cities.** Among the 20 largest, people live in *cooler*-than-average squares in Jaipur, Nagpur, Indore, Pune, Surat, Mumbai and Kozhikode. This matches the pilot, where bare outskirts heat up more than the city by day (Jodhpur). It is not yet tested by rainfall; that needs Role 2's `city_climate.csv`.
- **Gaps are small on average** (tenths of a degree) **but reach about 1 °C** in some cities (Kolkata +1.08 °C pre-monsoon day). Kolkata is also the city where crowded squares are least green (−0.05 NDVI).
- **Comparison with the literature:** Chakraborty et al. 2019 found poorer neighbourhoods hotter in 72% of 25 cities, using surface temperature. We use population density, not income, so we can say "more crowded squares", not "poorer". Our 63–87% by day is in the same range.
